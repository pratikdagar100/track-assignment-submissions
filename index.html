How to Collect Assignments Through One Link Instead of a Pile

Assignments arrive in every channel a class has: photos in the group chat, files by email, paper on the desk, a message saying “I sent it to your other number”. Marking starts with an hour of finding and sorting. One link per assignment ends that, because the work arrives already sorted. The change is small for students and large for you, and it goes smoothly if the first week is handled deliberately.

About 5 minutes to read · One task to switch over

What scattered collection actually costs

It is not only the hour of sorting. Work in a group chat has no name on it and can be deleted by the sender. Work by email lands between everything else. Paper cannot be searched. When a parent asks whether the homework was handed in, you have to check four places and still not be sure. And none of it can be counted: you do not know who has not submitted until you have matched everything to the register by hand.

One link per assignment fixes all of this at once, because every submission arrives with a name, a roll number, a timestamp and the task it belongs to.

Step 1: One link per assignment, never one link for everything

The temptation is to make one “homework” link and use it all term. Do not. Submissions from different tasks in one list are the pile again, just digital. Each assignment gets its own link, its own brief on the page, and its own deadline. The list behind each link then contains only that task’s work, and “who has not submitted” is a count, not a search.

Step 2: Make every submission identify itself

The page should ask for register name, roll number and class before it accepts anything, and the brief should say to use the real ones. That single requirement is what turns a pile into a list. If a student submits under a nickname, ask them to resubmit correctly the first time it happens; it will not happen twice.

Step 3: Say how the work should look when it arrives

Most link submissions are photos of handwritten pages, and most unreadable ones are unreadable for the same reasons. Put three lines on the page and the problem largely goes away.

  1. 1
    One clear photo per page, taken from above in good light, not at an angle from a bed.
  2. 2
    Name and question number on the page itself, so a photo that gets separated still makes sense.
  3. 3
    One file if possible, combined pages rather than six separate images, and a sensible file type.

Typed answers avoid all of this. Where the task allows typing, say it is preferred.

Step 4: Move the class over on one task, and close the old routes

Choose one assignment and announce that it is collected through the link only. Say it in the lesson, in the chat, and on the page. For that first task, accept work that comes the old way, but reply to each with “please put this through the link” and do not mark it until it is there. By the second task, the old routes are quiet.

Do not run both systems in parallel for a term. That is the pile plus a link.

Step 5: Mark from the list, and use the counts

With everything in one list, mark in whatever order suits you, and the list keeps track of what is done. The day before the deadline, the list of submitted names against the register is your reminder message, by name. After the deadline, the count of late and missing is a fact rather than an estimate, and it is the same fact when a parent asks.

Keep the lists. A term of them is the record of who handed in what, when, without a spreadsheet.

Worked example

Switching a class of 32 from chat to a link

Before: homework arrived as photos in the class chat, some by email, some on paper. Sorting took about forty minutes per task, and three students were regularly marked missing who had in fact sent something somewhere.

The switch: one assignment announced as link-only, with the identity and photo rules on the page. Seven students sent photos to the chat anyway; each was asked to resubmit through the link and did.

Second task: 30 of 32 through the link by the deadline, two reminded by name, both in the next day.

Sorting time: zero. The three “missing” students turned out to have been submitting all along.

Before you share it

  • Each assignment has its own link and its own list.
  • The page requires register name, roll number and class.
  • The page says how photographed work should look, and prefers typed answers where possible.
  • The switch was announced on one task and old routes were closed rather than run alongside.
  • Reminders and missing-work counts come from the list, by name.
